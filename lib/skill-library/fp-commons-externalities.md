# Reason About Commons and Externalities

Use this method when individual actions consume a shared resource or impose benefits and costs on others. Derive the resource and incentive problem before choosing a rule. Compare arrangements by how they change behavior under the actual monitoring and enforcement conditions.

## Primitives

Identify the resource stock or service capacity, its replenishment, and each withdrawal or use. Determine whether one person's use reduces what remains and whether access can be controlled. Separate private costs and benefits from effects on other participants. Rules require information, administration and a way to handle exceptions.

## Procedure

1. Define the resource boundary, participants and time scale. For a replenishing stock, write next stock as current stock plus replenishment minus withdrawals and losses.
2. Identify who can act, who benefits, who pays and who experiences congestion or depletion. Include participants absent from the immediate transaction.
3. Describe the current rules in practice: entry, allocation, monitoring, sanctions, appeals and rule revision. Do not substitute the formal policy for observed behavior.
4. Model plausible responses to candidate rules such as reservations, prices, limits, shared scheduling or participant agreements. Explain the response mechanism instead of assuming compliance.
5. Calculate administrative and information requirements. A theoretically precise quota may fail when usage cannot be measured reliably.
6. Test distribution and adaptation. Determine which participants lose access, can evade the rule, or can shift costs elsewhere.
7. Propose a bounded intervention and an observable outcome. Monitor resource condition and completed useful work, not only rule compliance.

## Worked example

Four teams share a GPU with 100 usable hours per week. Their requested hours total 140. A first-come queue encourages early reservations, including reservations later abandoned. Equal quotas of 25 hours prevent overbooking but can leave capacity unused when one team has little work.

A candidate rule grants each team a baseline allocation and returns unused reservations to a shared pool before their scheduled start. Measure completed jobs, idle reserved hours and urgent-job waiting time. If teams conceal their unused capacity to preserve future allocations, revise that incentive. The capacity arithmetic identifies scarcity; observed behavior determines whether the proposed allocation works.

## Output and check

Produce the resource balance, participant effects, existing rules, candidate mechanisms and outcome measures. Check that withdrawals and replenishment use the same units and that the intervention's monitoring is feasible.

## Failure boundary

A shared resource does not imply inevitable depletion, and private ownership or centralized control is not automatically superior. Institutional performance depends on local conditions. Keep empirical assumptions visible and update them from observed behavior.
