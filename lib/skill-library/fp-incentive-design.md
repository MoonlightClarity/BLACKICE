# Derive Incentive Responses

Use this method when a reward, penalty, contract or objective is expected to make another actor produce an outcome. Analyze the choices available to that actor. A desired outcome and a rewarded signal are different objects, even when they are correlated before incentives change.

## Primitives

Identify the principal's objective, the actor's feasible actions, the actor's costs and preferences, the observed signals and the reward rule. Information may be asymmetric. Several tasks can compete for limited effort. Actors may care about reputation, autonomy, relationships and future options as well as money.

## Procedure

1. State the actual outcome and the party whose objective it represents. Identify other affected objectives rather than silently combining them.
2. List the actor's available actions, including substitutes, shortcuts, delayed effort and legitimate refusal to participate. Include constraints on what the actor can control.
3. Specify exactly what is observed and how the reward changes with it. Distinguish noisy outcomes from directly controllable actions.
4. Compare the actor's payoff across plausible actions. Use ordinal rankings when numerical utilities would be invented. Explain which action becomes more attractive under the proposed rule.
5. Inspect task substitution. Determine what valuable but weakly measured activity may lose effort.
6. Modify the arrangement where necessary: change the measure, observation period, task boundary, feedback or reward strength. Preserve the underlying objective and operational feasibility.
7. Evaluate the mechanism with observed behavior. Look for changes in outcome, effort allocation and participation, not only improvement in the rewarded score.

## Worked example

A support organization rewards agents for closing tickets quickly. Agents can solve an issue, close it prematurely, or spend time on a difficult case. Faster closure is rewarded immediately; reopened cases are assigned elsewhere.

The mechanism favors closing before resolution because the cost of reopening is detached from the original action. Counting reopened incidents against the original cohort and measuring successful resolution after a suitable interval changes that tradeoff. It still does not establish a perfect metric: difficult cases may remain unattractive. Review case mix and preserve a route for legitimate complex work instead of treating every long ticket as poor performance.

## Output and check

Return an action-observation-reward table, predicted responses, the proposed mechanism and observable evaluation criteria. Confirm that each predicted response is feasible for the actor and that preferences are evidenced or explicitly assumed.

## Failure boundary

Do not infer that every actor maximizes a simple monetary utility or has malicious intent. An incentive model is conditional. Use measured behavior to test it, and distinguish a theoretical equilibrium from an established prediction.
