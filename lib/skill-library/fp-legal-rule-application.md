# Apply Legal Rules to Supported Facts

Use this method when a legal question risks being answered from a broad principle, remembered rule or preferred outcome. Identify the applicable authority and translate its requirements into a structured application to supported facts. Keep law, private terms, organizational policy and personal preference distinct.

## Primitives

A legal rule applies within a jurisdiction, time period and factual scope. Its operative structure may contain required elements, balancing factors, exceptions and procedural conditions. Authority can have different binding force. A factual allegation, established fact and legal conclusion are different inputs.

## Procedure

1. State the issue precisely and establish the relevant jurisdiction and date. Identify which missing details can change the applicable rule; use explicit alternatives when the available facts permit useful analysis.
2. Retrieve current primary authority, such as the enacted text, applicable regulation, controlling decision or actual contract. Verify effective dates, amendments and relevant procedural posture.
3. Identify the rule's source and force. Separate binding authority from persuasive commentary and distinguish a platform term or internal policy from a legal prohibition.
4. Parse the rule into elements or factors, exceptions and dependencies. Preserve conjunctions, alternatives, definitions and burden allocation instead of paraphrasing away decisive details.
5. Map supported facts to each requirement. Explain the connection, material contrary facts and the strongest relevant alternative interpretation.
6. Use cases by comparing legally significant facts and the reason for the decision. A similar outcome or vivid factual resemblance alone does not establish the analogy.
7. State the likely application, its basis and the specific factual or authority question that could change it. Complete the analysis supported by the available evidence rather than turning every uncertainty into a generic request for more information.

## Worked example

A hypothetical contract allows termination if delivery is more than ten days late and written notice remains uncured for five days. Delivery is twelve days late, but notice was sent yesterday.

The lateness condition appears satisfied; the cure-period condition does not yet follow from the stated facts. Whether days means calendar or business days, how notice becomes effective and whether another clause supplies an exception must be checked in the actual contract. A statement that late delivery permits immediate termination would erase an operative condition.

## Output and check

Return an issue-rule-fact table, cited primary authority, competing applications where material and a conclusion tied to the decisive conditions. Check every legal proposition against its source and date.

## Failure boundary

First-principles reasoning can expose the structure of a rule but cannot invent the governing law. Do not treat a fairness intuition, customary practice or private platform policy as controlling legal authority.
