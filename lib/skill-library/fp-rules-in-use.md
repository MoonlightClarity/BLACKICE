# Identify Rules in Use

Use this method when written policy does not explain what people actually do. Reconstruct the operative rules from decisions, incentives and enforcement. Preserve the distinction between what a rule authorizes, what participants believe and what routinely happens.

## Primitives

An institution assigns participation, permitted actions, information, rewards, sanctions and ways to resolve disputes. A rule has an author, audience, scope and enforcement mechanism. Repeated practice can differ from a formal instruction without acquiring the same authority. Exceptions may be deliberate, tolerated or unnoticed.

## Procedure

1. Define the recurring activity and outcome to explain. Select actual cases rather than beginning with a comprehensive organization chart.
2. Read the applicable formal rules and identify their scope, date and issuing authority. Preserve conflicts instead of silently choosing whichever text fits the story.
3. Trace observed decisions: who participated, what information they had, which actions were available and what happened afterward.
4. Identify enforcement and exceptions. Determine who notices violations, who can respond, which consequences follow and whether appeals or revisions occur.
5. Compare the formal and operative arrangements. Distinguish missing capability, ambiguous wording, conflicting incentives, selective enforcement and intentional delegation.
6. Explain the behavior through the identified mechanism. Predict a case in which that explanation would fail and seek the corresponding evidence.
7. Propose a change only at the level needed for the objective: clarify a rule, supply information, change incentives, transfer authority or revise the formal arrangement.

## Worked example

A shared equipment policy says reservations are first come, first served. In practice, one laboratory can displace any reservation by labeling its work urgent. Other teams begin reserving several backup slots.

The operative system includes an unbounded priority exception. The backups are a response to unreliable access, not necessarily a preference for wasting capacity. Compare documented urgent cases, actual displacement and unused backups. A revised arrangement could define an urgent capacity pool and make displaced time visible. Whether that change works depends on enforcement and participants' response, which must be observed.

## Output and check

Produce a rule map covering participation, action choices, information, allocation, enforcement, exceptions and revision. Link claims about practice to cases. Identify the mechanism that best explains the discrepancy.

## Failure boundary

Do not treat common practice as legal authority or infer deliberate wrongdoing from inconsistency alone. A written rule is evidence about formal expectations, not proof of implementation. Avoid universal claims that informal or formal arrangements are inherently better.
