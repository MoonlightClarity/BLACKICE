# Trace Policy Incidence and Responses

Use this method when a policy's effects are described solely by who is legally charged or who initially receives a benefit. Trace how participants adjust and where costs and benefits ultimately fall. Separate accounting identities from behavioral predictions.

## Primitives

The statutory payer, transaction participant and final bearer of a cost can differ. Prices, quantities, quality, timing and participation can change. Incidence depends on alternatives and constraints over a specified horizon. Transfers between participants differ from resources consumed or lost.

## Procedure

1. Define the intervention, baseline, affected transactions and time horizon. Retrieve the actual current rule when analyzing a real policy.
2. Map the initial payment or obligation and every directly affected group. Identify who can change prices, quantities, location, quality or participation.
3. Trace the plausible behavioral responses and their constraints. Distinguish immediate fixed commitments from longer-term substitution or entry.
4. Build an accounting model that assigns payments consistently. Then add explicitly evidenced or assumed behavioral relationships; do not infer them from the identity alone.
5. Estimate or bound effects by group. Include indirect participants where the mechanism supports them, and avoid counting a transfer as both a social cost and a separate loss.
6. Examine heterogeneous effects and spillovers. An average benefit can conceal losses for groups facing different alternatives.
7. Report scenarios and the assumption that most changes the distribution. Identify what observation would distinguish those scenarios.

## Worked example

A hypothetical transaction has a buyer price of 10 before a fee. A new fee of 2 is remitted by the seller. After adjustment, the buyer pays 11.50, and the seller retains 9.50 before other costs.

For each continuing unit, the buyer bears 1.50 and the seller bears 0.50 relative to the baseline. This allocation follows from the stated prices; it does not explain why those prices emerged. The explanation requires evidence about demand, supply and alternatives. If quantity falls, also analyze lost trades, changed resource use and the people who leave the market. The 75% versus 25% per-unit split is not the complete welfare result.

## Output and check

Return a flow-of-payments map, behavioral assumptions, group-level scenarios and the evidence needed to refine them. Verify that payments reconcile and that comparisons use the same baseline and period.

## Failure boundary

Do not equate legal responsibility with economic burden or assume full pass-through. Short-run responses may differ from long-run responses. Distributional analysis describes effects; choosing how to value those effects requires explicit objectives.
